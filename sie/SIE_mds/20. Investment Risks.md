**Systemic (Non-Diversifiable) Risk**: caused by factors that affect the prices of virtually all securities. Cannot be avoided by diversification
- **Market Risk**: day-to-day potential for an investor to experience losses due to market fluctuations in securities’ prices
	- **Beta** measures non diversifiable risk (volatility). S&P 500 has value of 1.0, >1 is more volatile, <1 is less volatile
- **Interest-Rate Risk**: changing interest rates affect existing bondholders. rising interest rates makes the value of existing bonds lower, since investors can obtain higher yields by purchasing newly issued bonds with higher coupon rates
	- sometimes to avoid this, diversity maturity dates, geographic locations, rates
	- all bonds have some exposure to interest-rate risk
	- **Duration**: Bonds with longer maturities and lower interest rates tend to be more vulnerable to interest-rate risk than bonds with shorter maturities. **Duration** measures the real change in a bonds price (1% change in interest rate for a 5yr bond affects price by 5%)
	- Equity for highly leveraged companies is also affected by interest rates
	- The price of preferred stocks (those that pay out dividends) are inversely affected by interest rates (higher rates cause fixed dividend payments to be worth less)
- **Inflation (Purchasing-Power) Risk**
	- experienced by investments that provide fixed payments (e.g., bonds and fixed annuities, if the dollar is worth less, the fixed payment is worth less
	- non-cash and non-fixed payment things don't experience it as much
	- Inflation hurts bondholders in two ways:
		1) inflation leads to rising interest rates which causes the market prices of their existing bonds to fall
		2) the purchasing power of their interest payments decreases.
	- **real rate of return**: rate of return MINUS inflation rate (CPI)
- **Event Risk**: terrorism, war, etc. - crashes the market

**Unsystematic (Diversifiable) Risk**
- **Alpha**: measures the risk that’s specific to a particular company
	- calculated by taking actual return MINUS expected return (Beta)
- **Business Risk**: a company may lose demand for its products or face increased comp
- **Regulatory Risk**: regulatory changes may affect business
- **Legislative Risk**: new laws may have a negative impact on an investment’s value
- **Political Risk**: investment may lose value due to changes in a country’s government or regulations (war, terrorism etc)
- **Liquidity Risk**: risk that investors may be unable to dispose of a securities position quickly and at a price that’s reasonably related to recent transactions (YOU CANT CASH OUT)
	- Increases with lower trading volume
	- Real estate, non market traded equities, hedge funds have low liquidity 
- **Opportunity (Cost) Risk**: Risk that selected investment/technique returns less than some alternative investment
- **Reinvestment Risk**: Risk that an investor will not be able to reinvest her principal at the same interest rate after a bond matures or is called. This situation typically occurs when interest rates have fallen.
- **Currency (Exchange-Rate) Risk**: the possibility that foreign investments will be worth less in the future due to changes in exchange rates
	- may also impact the price of a company that’s based in the U.S. if it earns revenue in a foreign country
- **Capital Risk**: risk that an investor could lose all or a portion of her investment
	- mostly faced by options investors as options could expire worthless
- **Credit Risk**: the risk that a bond issuer will not make payments as promised
	- U.S Treasuries are assumed to have virtually no credit (default) risk
- **Call Risk**: the risk that an issuer may decide to pay back its bondholders prior to maturity. Bonds are typically called when interest rates fall; therefore, bondholders receive their money back early and are unable to earn the same return when searching for a replacement investment.
- **Prepayment Risk**: risk is tied to homeowners paying off their mortgages early. When interest rates fall, homeowners have an incentive to refinance and pay off their existing mortgages

**Diversification Strategies**
- ### Buy-and-Hold
	- No changes to asset allocation
	- transaction costs and tax consequences are minimized since there’s no selling or purchasing
	- as the asset mix of the portfolio drifts, its risk/reward characteristics are altered
- ### Portfolio Rebalancing
	- involves a process of buying and selling assets on a periodic basis.
	- the original strategic asset allocation—and its risk/reward characteristics—may be restored (either periodically based on time or based on value, if a asset class drifts too far from original balance)
	- more frequent rebalancing will result in higher transaction costs as some assets are sold and others are purchased
These two approaches assume that markets are efficient (meaning its impossible to time changes in asset balances to take advantage of market movements)

**Indexing**
- involves either maintaining investments in companies that are part of major stock (or bond) indexes or investing in index funds directly (like DJIA, S&P 500, the S&P 400, or the Russell 2000)
- actively managed fund attempts to outperform a relevant index through superior stock-picking techniques; however, the composition of an index changes infrequently
- Index funds have lower trading expenses and fewer tax liabilities

**Active Strategies**
- **Sector Rotation**
	- Reallocating investments to different sectors based on what you think will perform better (timing the market/business cycle)
- **Dollar Cost Averaging**
	- a person invests a fixed-dollar amount at regular intervals, regardless of the market price of the security
	- designed to take the emotion out of the investment process and accepts that markets are subject to erratic swings
**Hedging**: (protection) essentially involves the client buying insurance to guard against the market moving against her.
- **Equity Options**: Can be used as a hedge against a equity position (if you own a stock you can buy a put option that increases in value if stock price reduces)
- **Index Options**: Instead of just a single equity, you can buy options to hedge against downturns in a whole market
- **Currency Options**: Companies may buy currency options to hedge against rise/decline in certain currencies. For example, if a company pays for goods in Euros, they may buy euro calls since the options will become more valuable if the euro does rise in value
	- There are no straight options for USD, so its USD vs another currency
	- Settled in USD
