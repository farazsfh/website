**Bond**: contract between issuer and investor 
- investor lends money to issuer, issuer promises to repay *debt service* (total of all interest payments over the bond’s life AND principal)
Raising money through debt is called **leverage financing** (borrowing against net worth)

**Par value**: amount investor expects to receive at maturity (bonds are usually issued in $100s, except US treasury which can be $100 or less)

**Coupon Rate**: interest rate of the bond, calculated based on par value
- Usually fixed for life of bond, but some _variable or floating rate securities_ have changing rates

**Yield**: total interest payments from a bond
- Interest is usually paid out semi-annually, once at maturity, and once 6 months before that date

Traditionally, bonds pay interest on the 1st or 15th of the month. If a bonds first coupon is for more than 6 months, its called a long coupon. If its less than 6 months, its a short coupon.

If a bond is sold before interest payments, the investor is entitled to interest accrued thus far.

For calculation purposes, corporate and municipal bonds use 30 days in every month and 360 days in the year, while U.S. government T-notes and T-bonds use actual days in every month and 365 days in the year.

**Zero-Coupon Bonds**: Don't pay periodic interest, but pay a lump sum greater than value bough for at maturity

**Maturity Date**: date on which the bondholder will receive the $1,000 return of principal from the issuer.

**Term Issue**: When a corporation releases bonds that mature on the same date
**Serial Issue**: When a corporation staggers bond maturity dates
**Balloon maturity**: some of it matures serially, large portion at final maturity

**Price fluctuations**
- Bonds usually sell for par value, if it sells for less on the market, its at a *discount*, if it sells for more, its at a *premium*
- **Interest-Rate Risk**: If interest rates go up, existing bonds have to be discounted since investors can get better yields with new bonds (since coupon rate and interest rate will get closer) - same conversely
	- Long term bonds are more affected by interest rates since money is locked up for longer
- **Credit Risk**: If the credit worthiness of a issuer is doubted, it has to sell its bonds at a discount to attract investors
	- US Government securities have 0 credit risk
	- Investors use credit rating companies (_Moody’s,_ _Standard and Poor’s (S&P),_ and _Fitch Investors Service_) to determine a corporations credit rating

A bond’s price is usually stated as a percentage of its par value.
- A price of 90 with a par value of 1000 means it costs $900.
- A price of 100 with a par value of 1000 means it costs $1000.
It's not always even, traditionally, corporate and municipal bonds trade in increments of 1/8 of a point, while Treasury notes and bonds trade in increments of 1/32 of a point.

**Call Provisions**: allows the _issuer_ to redeem its outstanding bonds before they reach maturity
- Investor receives par plus any accrued interest 
- Callable bonds benefit the issuer since they can stop periodic payments and take advantage of declining interest rates, since they can re-issue at lower coupon
- **Call protection**: period where bond cannot be called
- **Call premium**: the premium the issuer pays to call back the bond (x amount above par)
- **In-whole calls**: the whole series is called *MUST BE DISCLOSED*
- **Partial (lottery)** calls: a part of the series is called *MUST BE DISCLOSED*
- **Catastrophe** calls: calling a bond if the underlying collateral is wiped out (doesnt need to be disclosed)

**Put Provisions**: Gives the *bondholder* the right to redeem the bond on a specified date prior to maturity
- Usually have lower yields since bondholders can redeem bonds when interest rates rise

**Convertible Bonds**: an incentive where an investor may convert the par value of the bond into a predetermined number of shares of the companies common stock
- Usually offer lower coupons
- Usually offered by corps with weak credit ratings
- **Conversion price**: price per share of the converted bond
- **Conversion ratio**: number of shares received when converting par value to equity
- Benefit for issuer = can offer lower coupon
	- conversion adjusts the mandatory debt obligation into equity and deleverages the corporation’s balance sheet
- Benefit for investor = has inherent value bc bond can be converted
- **Forced conversion**: when a convertible bond is called, and its more beneficial for the investor to convert
- CONVERSION IS NOT TAXABLE
